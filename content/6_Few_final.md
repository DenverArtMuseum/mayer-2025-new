In late fall 1705, a Maya healer named María García treated a female servant after the woman experienced a sudden and unexplained onset of blindness. The servant, named Felipa Jérez, described as a woman of Black, Maya, and Spanish descent (*mulata*), lived and worked in her employer’s house located just off the main plaza in the capital city, Santiago de Guatemala. García made a living as a *curandera*, an unlicensed healer, with a reputation for treating eye and stomach illnesses. She attracted patients from across the region, who paid for her services in money or goods. She also ministered to women experiencing difficult pregnancies, which made her a midwife as well. García traveled to treat patients where they lived and, when needed, nursed the sick back to health in her own home in Ciudad Vieja. García’s healing practices, like those of other unlicensed Maya healers at that time, drew on intersecting medical cultures circulating in eighteenth-century Guatemala: Mesoamerican curing and botanical knowledge, Spanish and African medicine, and Catholic traditions of health and healing.[^1]

Locating and interpreting archival evidence describing colonial Maya women healers and midwives like María García present many challenges. Descriptions of women’s lives are rare in colonial archives, and archival documents written by women, or even just capturing their words and perspectives, are rarer still. This is especially the case for Maya women, free and enslaved Black women, and mixed-race women. What historians can uncover about García’s life comes only from a handful of Inquisition testimonies recorded as part of a legal case against her that took place between 1705 and 1713, when Inquisition authorities prosecuted her for being a healer who used the “arts of spell casting, enchantments \[and\] charms.”[^2] This makes the task of reconstructing the lives of healers and midwives like García difficult because information about them appears primarily through legal testimonies given by their patients, relatives, neighbors, and employers that were shaped not only by the legal system but also the interpersonal conflicts, rife with racial, ethnic, and gender stereotypes that framed everyday social life.[^3]

When interpreting García’s work and life, and the lives of others through Inquisition proceedings, researchers must constantly keep in mind that testimony took place in the context of a coercive Inquisition trial, recorded by scribes who generally offered only summaries of testimony based on their notes and not a verbatim transcript of what was said. When Maya and other Indigenous persons came before the courts as witnesses or the accused, those who did not speak and understand Spanish spoke through an interpreter in *lengua,* in Kaqchikel, Kʹicheʹ, or other Mayan languages that Inquisition scribes did not themselves understand. Nevertheless, the details of María García’s life** **gleaned from Inquisition testimony underscore the important, but often unacknowledged roles, that Maya women healers and midwives played in the fabric of daily life in their communities and in multi-ethnic colonial societies in Spanish America.[^4]

At the turn of the eighteenth century, María García lived in the small pueblo of Ciudad Vieja, populated mainly by Kaqchikel and Nahuatl speakers.[^5] The town was located on the outskirts of Santiago de Guatemala the capital of colonial Central America, which is today known as Antigua and is a UNESCO designated World Heritage site.[^6] The city had some thirty thousand residents at that time, about 15 percent of residents were classified as Spanish and another 15 percent as Indigenous, with the remaining 70 percent included free and enslaved Black people and persons with various racial lineages categorized as *castas*. Santiago’s status as the regional political and religious capital marked the urban landscape: The city was constructed in a grid pattern with the main buildings of political and religious authority on or near the central plaza: the *audiencia* (royal) and Inquisition courts, the Cathedral and Archbishop’s palace, the principal headquarters of the missionary orders and the cathedral. Elite residents lived as close as they could to this center, and so proximity to it acted as a marker of high social status. The central city was ringed by barrios, or neighborhoods, each with their own parish church. Santiago contained four monasteries for men (Mercedarians, Dominicans, Franciscans, and Jesuits) and fifteen convents for women including those dedicated to Santa Catalina Mártir, Santa Teresa, and Santa Clara. Founded in the mid-seventeenth century, the Universidad de San Carlos included a medical school from the beginning, with four hospitals dotting the city. The first printing press arrived in the 1660s, and by 1700, and the time of García’s Inquisition trial, there was a thriving book industry that published works by Guatemalan authors, as well as works of Europeans and others in translation.[^7]

The Inquisition came to the Americas in New Spain in 1532, where it was run until the early 1570s, first by members of the missionary orders and later by the bishop of Mexico. In this early period, investigations mainly targeted newly converted Indigenous elites for heresy and returning to Indigenous religious practices. In 1571, the Spanish Crown established the formal Holy Office of the Inquisition in Spanish America and set up a professional bureaucracy of inquisitors, positions filled by men from Spain. The highest Inquisition court in the viceroyalty was located in Mexico City and had jurisdiction over all of New Spain. Sub-Inquisition courts were established in major colonial cities. Santiago de Guatemala’s Inquisition court was established in 1572 with jurisdiction over colonial Central America.[^8]

As part of establishing and maintaining order, the Spanish Crown created the legal systems and bureaucracies to prosecute witchcraft as part of religious conversion campaigns and social control efforts of colonized peoples that began with the wars of conquest and continued through the wars of independence in the early nineteenth century. The Inquisition was just one such effort . Its investigations, prosecutions, and correspondence prove a significant site for detailed information about Maya and African curing and medicine as officials devoted time and resources to policing sorcery, witchcraft, and magical healing. Parish priests and other religious officials encouraged the faithful, from the pulpit and in the confessional, to denounce anyone suspected of suspicious activities, pitting family members, neighbors, local business owners, and other women against each other. Testimonies reflected racial, ethnic, and gender stereotypes and hierarchies found in early modern colonial witch beliefs, inextricably linked to popular understandings of Christianity and of the ways that God and Satan operated in the world. These beliefs influenced the world views about the religious and ritual practices of colonial peoples, including María García.

The denunciation to the Inquisition against García for sorcery and the subsequent witness interviews open a window not only into her medical practice but also to the tensions within women’s multi-ethnic social relations in colonial society. The trial proceeded sporadically, beginning in 1705 and ending in 1713 when García was finally questioned by Inquisition authorities in Santiago. She was a community elder in her late fifties, the widow of an Indigenous soap maker. Under questioning during her trial, García confirmed that she was indeed a curandera and that she had been practicing her calling for many years. The coversheet for the case materials, housed today in the Archivo General de la Nación in Mexico City, shows that the case began in 1705, and that the official charge was *por curandera y embustera* (for being a healer and a liar) (fig. 1). The Inquisition here described García as a mestiza but in other places as Indigenous. Legally, Mayas and other Indigenous peoples were not allowed to be tried by the Inquisition because they were deemed new converts and so had to be tried through the ecclesiastical court system. In other words, if the authorities categorized García as mestiza, the trial could continue. Some witnesses described her as Indigenous and others as mestiza, perhaps meaning that she was considered to have Maya and Spanish heritage, or that she was bilingual or at least spoke and understood some Spanish, or that she may have worn what was considered more European-style dress.[^9]

The case itself centered on a strange, possibly supernatural event. Felipa Jérez, described as “blind with a broken throat” (*ciega de la garganta quebrada*), was a *mulata* weaver and laundress who worked as a servant in an elite home located near the city’s central plaza. One night, a strange woman peddler, dressed in mourning veil covering her head and face, knocked on the kitchen door and offered some items for sale. Jérez declined to purchase anything. After she closed the door and made her way back to the kitchen, she felt intense pain in her head and suddenly lost her sight. She took to her bed and was unable to work. Jérez’s blindness was incurable until María García treated her with healing herbs.[^10]

Though Jérez was home alone that evening, cooking a hot chocolate drink in the kitchen, when the peddler knocked on the door, a number of other women who provided Inquisition testimony about the events repeated essentially the same details. The women included Doña Juana de Aragón, the Spanish widow of an *alférez*, a high-ranking councilor in the *audiencia*, who employed Jérez as a servant, and her cousin and neighbor Doña Isabel Sanchez de Léon, a thirty-two-year-old Spanish woman married to a merchant. In fact, it was Doña Isabel who first denounced García to Inquisition authorities for superstitious healing. Jérez’s sisters, who also lived and worked nearby, testified in detail about the events in their Inquisition testimony: Cecilia de Cuellar, a thirty-year-old single mestiza who worked as a kitchen servant in nearby household, and Luciana de Jérez, who described herself as Felipa Jérez’s sister, a widowed mestiza of fifty years old. Because personal details about women like these rarely emerge from colonial sources, it is rare to see two overlapping family support networks, one elite with Doña Juana and her younger cousin Doña Isabel and the other among Felipa Jérez and her two sisters who all worked for a living. And we only have partial accounts of women’s lives for only a limited time period when they do emerge from the archives. Despite the fact that these women lived in different colonial worlds, separated by social status and ethnicity, they did agree on this: The circumstances of Jérez’s sudden blindness, the inability of anyone including a medical doctor to cure it, and what they understood from Jérez to be the circumstances of María García’s cure together indicated that supernatural activities were involved.[^11]

Felipa Jérez eventually testified before the Inquisition about her blindness and María García’s cure. Jérez can only be described as a reluctant witness. She did not, herself, denounce García to the Inquisition; Jérez testified only when she was formally called to appear before the court, something that would have been terrifying to anyone. Perhaps she was grateful to García because it seems that García was the only person who was able to heal her after three months of blindness and head pain. Admittedly, however, the circumstances that she shared with her family and employer about the night the García came to her house and took Jérez back to her home in Ciudad Vieja to treat her there, invited speculations of sorcery, details that travelled *por pública voz y fama*—the neighborhood’s rumor network.[^12]

Jérez remembered that the illness began August 25, 1705, the day of San Luiz Rey de Francia, and was cured on the eve of the day of San Francisco Xavier, December2, 1705. People often recalled the dates of certain events based on the saint’s day or holiday on which they occurred. Doña Juana de Aragón, Jérez’s employer, treated her with medicines she had on hand at home but could not cure her blindness, nor could the medical doctor called to the house. Jérez described how she could no longer work as a servant and so was forced to work doing laundry at the public fountain. Because she was blind, she used a young girl as a guide and assistant. One day at the public fountain, María García approached her and said she had a *remedio* (cure) for her blindness. Perhaps Jérez knew of García’s medical reputation for treating eyes, or maybe she just hoped that García could offer some relief. Jérez decided to sell what belongings she had to pay for the cure.[^13]

Early in the evening on December 1, María García met Jérez as planned at Doña Juana’s home. Jérez described herself as worried about entrusting herself to García, so she protected her person with religious objects (*cargada de reliquias*). García led the blind women to her home in Ciudad Vieja. When they arrived and entered the house, Jérez felt García place her on a chair. The curandera said, “Let me see your eyes” then put some “very strong powders” in them. A medicinal bath followed, scented with herbs and plant materials. Then García lightly wrapped bandages around her eyes. Jérez told authorities that the treatments made her feel *atarantada*, dazed or stunned.[^14]

In her testimony, Jérez framed time, memory, her strange blindness, and eventual cure through cultures of religion and the role the supernatural played in early eighteenth-century Guatemalan society. After García allowed Jérez to rest a bit after her treatments, the curandera then accompanied Jérez on a night-time journey from Ciudad Vieja back to her home. Jérez, with the bandages still covering her eyes, recounted it to the Inquisition as a strange supernatural-tinged trip where, along the way, her sight slowly came back. They took a circuitous route together: They passed by the Alameda and the Campo del Calvario, then by the convento of the nuns of La Concepción. At some point, Jérez tripped over the walls around the Ermita Santa Lucía (the church devoted to the patron saint of the blind). Because her sight was returning, Jérez could tell exactly where she was. At some points along the way, the servant described feeling “like she was flying through the air” and that she “saw bulls dance,” elements in depictions of early modern European witches’ sabbaths. Once, as they neared the city, Jérez thought that she could see in the distance a strange woman dressed all in white, but she could not make out her face. Finally, they walked through the central plaza crossing in front of Santiago’s cathedral, and then García took Jérez to the home of one of her sisters.

The fact that the medicines from the García’s treatment made Jérez feel confused or enter some sort of altered state worried the Inquisition authorities, who often associated reports like this as evidence of witchcraft. Doña Isabel related in her testimony that when María García dropped off Jérez at her sister’s house after being treated, the woman was indeed dazed, using the same word *atarantada*, that Jérez used in her own testimony. Doña Isabel added that, while she had begun to recover some of her sight, Jérez remained *incensata* (lacking in reason) and *aturdida* (confused) for some hours after. Jérez’s sister Cecilia confirmed that the night that Jérez returned, García’s medicines seemed to be working, that Jérez was recovering her sight, and that she seemed stunned or dazed from the medicines. The Inquisition concluded the first round of questioning by late spring of 1706 and then compiled the documents together and sent them to the Viceregal Inquisition authorities in Mexico City.[^15]

For some reason not explained, authorities in Mexico did not respond until seven years later, in 1713, with a letter instructing the court in Guatemala to continue the case. Officials quickly arrested María García, but Felipa Jérez could not be found, having apparently fled the capital, and so could not be requestioned about the events. García’s interrogation focused on her medical practice, her training, and on her use of healing herbs and other ingredients. When asked how she learned the healing arts and why she was so successful, García testified that she had been a curandera for many years and that her healing methods centered on the use of a medicinal plant called *tesumpate*. Gabriel Varaona, an Indigenous man, taught her the properties of this plant and how to identify it, harvest it, and prepare it for different uses. The questioning then turned to why so many people from different social groups in the region consulted her for cures. García answered that they came and asked her to treat them because of her successful reputation and that she “always healed people who came to her house, especially those that had illnesses of the stomach and eyes.” The central medicinal ingredient of her practice was the tesumpate plant, which she used “to cure people, or perhaps she mixed in some medicines from the *boticario* (one of the official, regulated drug stores in the capital).” García denied teaching anyone else how to cure and denied as well that she made a “pact with the Devil to obtain her healing skillseither to cure someone or learn how to cure; the Indigenous \[man\] Gabriel de Varaona taught her what she knows.”[^16]

Questioners then switched topics and focused on the illnesses that García treated and whether or not she had treated anyone who claimed that their illness had supernatural origins or was cast by a healer hired by an enemy or rival who specialized in causing illnesses. García replied that yes, she remembered in particular curing the Franciscan priest Padre Fray José Vélez who told her that he had been bewitched (*estaba hechisado*). She gave him a curative drink “made from powders of the said plant tesumpate, and he was healed.” It is interesting here that García admitted to treating patients convinced that a sorcery spell caused their illness and that the person she named to the court was a missionary priest from the Franciscan order because it shows that while some priests acted as part of the Inquisition to prosecute magic, other priests used magical specialists in their personal lives.

While García testified in court, two men working for the Inquisition broke into her home and searched it for suspicious items, medicines, and materials associated with sorcery. They found and catalogued Garciá’s healing supplies and tools, which included a mix of undescribed religious items, amulets, a large gourd cup “filled with diverse kinds of roots, herbs, religious relics,” and “paper packets of seeds” stored in small gourds or wrapped in cotton.

Tesumpate remains to be identified. García claimed under oath that it was simply “a common plant,” a “thorny rose-colored bush with purplish black berries” that grew in and around the valley of Guatemala. The plant had no supernatural or dangerous qualities, she argued, which may or may not have been what she really thought or knew. The Inquisition scribe may have garbled the name of the plant when recording testimony; it is not a word in Spanish and was likely an Indigenous language term for the plant. Or perhaps the word *tesumpate* may be a Hispanicization of a plant called in Nahuatl *tezompahtli* (written also as *tezonpahtli* or *tezompatli*). This plant can be found in Francisco Hernández’s sixteenth-century compendium of Indigenous medical-botanical knowledge. Hernández, however, claims the plant is useful in treating coughs and does not mention stomach illnesses. After deliberation and reviewing the testimony and material evidence, the Inquisition ordered García to stop her work as a curandera, the main way of supporting herself as a widowed woman and elder, or face banishment from Ciudad Vieja.[^17]

The Inquisition’s prosecution of the Maya healer and midwife María García in eighteenth-century Santiago de Guatemala shows how the Catholic Church and the inquisitors, monitored and policed curing practices, medicinal plants, and other materials, especially those used by unlicensed healers and Indigenous, Black, and mixed-race women who practiced medical trades in the city. Maya, Nahua, and other Indigenous healers entered the archival record through Inquisition testimony through the Christian religious frameworks for understanding health, illness, and healing. Guatemala’s Inquisition attempted to counter Maya healers, especially women, by discounting them, calling them swindlers or liars, or for the more well-known healers, characterizing them as witches and prosecuting them, as in the case of María García.

The Inquisition sources documenting García’s medical practice provide evidence for how the colonial state deployed religious prosecution of medicine as part of the maintenance of Spanish colonial authority, encouraging men and women in colonial society, as part of their religious duty, to denounce suspicious healers and midwives to the Inquisition. At the same time, evidence from García’s life also provide an example of the ways Maya women healers and midwives healed and cared for others, roles they continue to have in their communities today, as well as their resilient methods of cultural survival despite efforts by the colonial state to erase or criminalize these practices.
