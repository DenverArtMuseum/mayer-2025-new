Maya women have ruled kingdoms and shaped dynasties. Today, Maya women manage household gardens, run weaving cooperatives, and work alongside their husbands in the *milpa* (maize field). They care for children and the elderly and advocate for women and children’s health in their *pueblos* (villages). Not surprisingly, Maya women are seen as keepers of traditional knowledge and practices. As a result, Maya women tend to be associated with domestic spaces and rural landscapes in the popular imagination.

Yet, Maya women also live and work in cities. In Mexico’s Yucatan Peninsula, Yucatec Maya women migrate to tourism centers like Cancun where they work primarily as domestic servants and hotel maids.[^1] By participating in labor migration, they gain access to much needed cash to supplement the income of rural households. Migration also disrupts local expectations that Maya women should remain in rural villages. In this essay, I spotlight Yucatec Maya women’s migrations and leadership roles in cities like Cancun, as a counterbalance to popular depictions of Maya women as bound to rural and domestic spaces.

I ask how has migration transformed Yucatec Maya women’s lives. These women remain actively involved in their pueblos, but they also take on new roles to make the city livable. Inspired by their pueblo’s quest for self-determination, Yucatec Maya women are leading the struggle for land rights in Cancun. Following the advice of their grandparents to “move forward . . . \[and\] never give up,” they demand Indigenous rights to the city and housing justice.[^2] By centering Yucatec Maya migrant women’s present-day struggles for political recognition, I foreground the visible and invisible practices they cultivate to combat precarity in the city. I show that for Maya women, these struggles point to the need for fostering sustainable tourism economies that equitably engage Indigenous communities. [^3]

**The Ethics of Ethnographic Representation**

As a Chicana feminist anthropologist, my research heeds the call to decolonize anthropology by refusing to treat Indigenous peoples as objects. A decolonial anthropology respectfully centers Indigenous experiences, while acknowledging the harms of previous colonial and anthropological projects. Sharing how my own positionality as a researcher and as a woman shapes my research is critical to this approach. Over the past three decades, I have collaborated with Maya communities in the Yucatan Peninsula. Since my goal is to produce research Maya communities can use to resist dispossession and displacement, I have been given permission by these communities to share their stories.

These collaborations focus on understanding Indigenous migrations and tourism economies in the Americas. Through this work, I have become attentive to the ways Maya communities, especially Maya women, have been marketed for tourist consumption. These images play up stereotypes of Maya women as “guardians of tradition” and folk objects, flattening the complexity of their lives.[^4] To challenge these stereotypes, I center Yucatec Maya present-day struggles for political recognition, economic equality, and housing justice. My books *A Return to Servitude: Maya Migration and the Tourist Trade in Cancún* (2010) and *Indigenous Dispossession: Housing and Maya Indebtedness in Mexico* (2021) are guided by educator Linda Tuhiwai Smith’s call to replace racialized narratives of Indigenous people with counter-stories of Indigenous resistance.[^5]

**Nature and Domesticity**

The tourist corridor of the Yucatan Peninsula is filled with artwork depicting “traditional” Maya life. This artwork caters to a popular imaginary that continues to place Maya women in domestic and rural spaces, aligning them closer to nature. For example, one of the statues in the central plaza of the city of Valladolid is of a *mestiza*, a Maya woman carrying a water jug and wearing a *huipil* (a white cotton embroidered dress typically worn by Maya women).[^6] Another example is of a mural located on the wall of a restaurant near the Cenote Dzalbay (fig. 1). It depicts a young Maya woman sitting at the hearth in her kitchen. Instead of wearing Western clothing which is common in rural pueblos, she wears a huipil, which today is primarily worn by elderly Maya women, and is surrounded by objects associated with Indigenous cultural practices—a *palapa* (thatched house with timber walls), a large *jícara* (dried gourd) used for storage, a *fogón* (hearth, called a *k′óoben* in Mayan) made up of a cast iron griddle placed on top of three rocks, and handmade tortillas. Moreover, she is surrounded by nature. Banana leaves and the tail of a *toh* (motmot) bird, which is known to live near cenotes, hover over her right shoulder, while next to the fogón is a *tejón* (coati) staring longingly at the tortillas. A third example can be found in the town of Espita, located an hour north of Valladolid. The main plaza is decorated with an outdoor mural that depicts a typical scene in a rural Maya village. Three mestizas, all wearing white huipiles, are hauling water from a well (fig. 2). A calabash tree sits in the background bursting with ripe jícaras ready to be harvested. One woman pulls water from the well, while another fills the large clay jugs. Another young woman cross-stitches while sitting at the base of the well.

Both murals depict domestic practices rooted in the past that are rarely practiced or even discontinued. For example, most pueblos now have running water, making hauling water no longer necessary. Cross-stitching is a skill that young women learned twenty years ago but is now uncommon since most women embroider huipiles with sewing machines. Additionally, after the Yucatecan government organized a campaign detailing the respiratory harms of smoke inhalation from open fire cooking in the early 2000s, fogones were replaced with gas stoves. They continue to be used but are built primarily outdoors when religious festivities call for special meals prepared over an open fire. By associating Maya women with these traditional practices, these women become symbols of an idealized past. To disrupt these narratives, I share counter-stories based on ethnographic work I have conducted in the Maya pueblo of Kuchmil.[^7]

**The Pueblo of Kuchmil**

Kuchmil is located in southeastern Yucatan. It was established in the 1890s by Maya families who were displaced during the Caste War (1847–1901).[^8] The Maya rebels fought for independence from the Mexican state and were devotees of the Speaking Cross, which prophesied that the rebels would win the war. Kuchmil is made up of approximately one hundred inhabitants who are *ejido* members. In an ejido system, land is owned communally; ejido members cannot sell the land but have the right to work the land in perpetuity. Ejido members cultivate corn, beans, and squash and are reliant on rain for irrigation. Since this system provides enough crops for a family’s basic consumption, Kuchmil residents participate in labor migration to augment household budgets. Soon after the tourist center of Cancun was established in the 1970s, Kuchmil residents began migrating there. Today more migrants from Kuchmil live in Cancun than in Kuchmil.

My first visit to Kuchmil took place in June 1991. Anthropologist James Fox arranged for me to hone my Yucatec Mayan language skills and develop an undergraduate research project in Kuchmil. I was told that due to my age—I was twenty years old—I would be limited to spaces occupied by young, unmarried women in the pueblo. Therefore, my research focused on studying adolescent life in the pueblo. I noted during this visit that Maya women and girls were encouraged to adhere to a model of “traditional” gender roles based on an agrarian system organized around a gendered division of labor. Men and boys were active in the public domain; they collected firewood and planted corn. Conversely, girls were restrained to the domestic sphere; they washed laundry, cooked, and cared for garden plants, chickens, and pigs. Marriage, for example, is a traditional role for men and women (fig. 3). In 1991, girls were expected to remain in Kuchmil until they married someone from a neighboring village. I was also told that it was not appropriate for young women to work outside the household because they could not be protected from harm—sexual and physical.

However, I quickly learned that in practice gender roles were far more flexible. For example, figure 3 shows one of these gendered practices: a Maya woman with an infant carrying corn to a public mill. As a young woman, I was expected to step into these “traditional” roles of caring for the household by hauling water and helping with domestic chores. Yet, as an anthropology student, I had some leeway and could disrupt these roles. For example, I was allowed to attend ejido meetings—meetings that were considered to be male-dominated spaces—to learn about local politics as long as I was chaperoned by a child or an adult. Maya girls were also given similar latitude, especially as migration became more and more prevalent.

**Internados (Boarding Schools)**

Education was one key way gender roles were disrupted and helped set the stage for women to migrate to Cancun.[^9] Prior to the 1960s, Maya families sent their sons to school but not their daughters. There were few schools located in or near Kuchmil, and teacher absenteeism was a longstanding problem in the local elementary school. The Ministry of Education (Secretaría de Educación Pública) and the National Indigenist Institute created *internados* (boarding schools) to provide rural students with access to elementary and junior high school educations. The internados claimed to provide a better-quality education for children in the countryside. Not surprisingly, beginning in the late 1960s through the 1970s, twenty-eight children (twenty-four boys and four girls) from Kuchmil enrolled in the internados. Like Native American boarding schools in the United States, the internados aimed to assimilate Maya students, although it’s very important to note that the internados in the Yucatan Peninsula were not as harsh and punitive as residential boarding schools in the US and Canada. Another goal of the internados was to create rural leaders. To that end, students were taught to read, write, and speak Spanish. They developed their leadership skills and studied agriculture, engineering, and art.

The majority of the boarding schools were located far from Kuchmil (fig. 5). For example, the *casa escuela* (boarding house) in Maxcanú, where doña Andrea May Citep sent her daughter, was a two-days walk from Kuchmil. The distance was a major deterrent. Parents also feared for their daughter’s physical safety living far from home. Only a few mothers in Kuchmil had attended school themselves; they advocated for their daughters to receive the same opportunity. Doña Andrea had not been educated as a young girl, but her husband insisted that their daughters “learn to write, learn to read.” She also proposed that one of her daughters attend a boarding school. When I asked how she dealt with fears over her daughter’s safety, doña Andrea explained, “She wasn’t little, she was ten years old, she washed her own clothes. . . .We couldn’t afford her books which is why we sent her to Maxcanú. I never cried for her because the girl was fine where she studied. I saw it \[the school\]. There was a bed, there was a hammock.” By the age of ten, girls in Kuchmil are taught how to do all basic domestic chores. They are seen as quite capable and responsible. More important, having a daughter who was educated was quite beneficial for local households and worth the risks associated with attending boarding schools.

The internados in Mexico took different forms. Some were residential schools, while others were structured as dorms located near public schools like the internado at Maxcanú. Some students disliked the internado because it was located far from home, and they couldn’t get used to living with strangers. For young girls, the internado was an opportunity to enjoy their childhood because it provided a respite from the humdrum of daily chores. One student, Celia, recalled the activities with great fondness. During the week, she attended and lived in the *albergue* (residential school) located in the neighboring town. “I was five years old when I went to the internado in Ke′eldzonot, from Monday through Friday. Only on Saturday and Sunday, I was at home. Come Monday very early, at five in the morning, we had to walk to school . . . .I really liked it. There we ate. They gave us everything . . . .We had physical education, carpentry, sewing, and even dance classes. Saturdays and Sundays they showed movies in one of the classrooms, of the old kind like *Cinderella.* Vendors would sell cookies. Every fifteen days they gave us an allowance, fifteen pesos.” Not surprisingly, Celia became one of the first girls to migrate to Cancun to work in the tourism industry in the 1980s.

Fifteen of the students who attended internados for elementary education eventually studied middle school at internados. One of these students was a girl. In this case, the girl’s parents moved to a larger town, so all their children could earn a vocational degree. This young woman became a schoolteacher and became a role model of a different kind of future. After a secondary school was built in the neighboring town in 1997, local families no longer needed to send their children to internados.

Today, all girls attend elementary and secondary school. Of those who graduate from high school, many pursue college degrees. These young women have become leaders of their pueblo.[^10] They work closely with NGOs to organize agrarian projects in the community and organize sewing cooperatives that sell embroidered textiles to the tourist market. They care for children and the elderly, manage household gardens, work alongside their husbands and sons in the milpa, and advocate for women and children’s health by serving as *promotoras* (advocates) for the mobile health clinic. They also organize religious processions and festivals in their community (fig. 6).

**Migration as Empowerment**

Agrarian economies are subsistence economies. To provide greater access to cash for their families, Yucatec Maya women also participate in labor migration. This practice began soon after my first visit to Kuchmil in 1991. When I returned the following summer to conduct follow-up interviews, I found that one-third of the young women in Kuchmil had migrated to work as domestic servants in neighboring cities of Valladolid, Tizimin, and Cancun. This unprecedented shift was due in part to the newly paved road which made it possible to establish a bus route. Traveling between Kuchmil and Valladolid became easy and safe. Since then, Yucatec Maya women have been migrating to tourism centers like Cancun where they work primarily as hotel maids and domestic servants.

To illustrate the ways Maya women have become key decision makers in their households and as community leaders, I share Mariela’s migration experience. Mariela did not attend an internado, although one was located near Kuchmil, because her parents feared for her safety while walking to and from there. As a result, Mariela did not study past fifth grade. Her parents argued that Mariela was needed at home to help care for her younger siblings and her mother who was ill. In contrast, Nicolás, her older brother, attended an internado from sixth through eighth grade. He eventually found work in Cancun.

In 1992, one year after the road was built that connected Kuchmil to Valladolid, Mariela’s father injured his arm and was unable to farm, placing the family in dire straits. When Nícolás came home to help his father with the crops, he gave up his job in Cancun, even though his wages subsidized the household. Mariela’s Aunt Luci proposed that eighteen-year-old Mariela work as a domestic servant for a family she knew in the city of Tizimin where she lived. At first, her parents refused but eventually relented. Mariela was excited to earn her own money. She was paid \$60 a month, of which \$15 to \$25 was sent to her parents.

Few young women left the community to find work. Since Mariela worked as a live-in domestic servant for strangers, she was labeled a “Guadalupe,” a name that referenced a character in a Mexican soap opera who moves to the big city to work as a domestic servant and ends up impregnated by the employer’s son. In response to these painful accusations, Mariela’s parents insisted that she return home. Mariela, however, refused to do so. Only when she became ill did she finally return home. Since her sister had married and her parents implored her to stay home, Mariela did so for three years. When she returned to Tizimin, Mariela opted to live with her aunt and was employed as a live-out domestic servant to squash rumors about her morals. One year later, she joined her siblings in Cancun. Her parents tried to convince Mariela to return home because they feared for her safety in Cancun, but Mariela had wised up. This time she persuaded them to join her in Cancun.

Mariela remained in Cancun even after her parents and younger sibling returned to Kuchmil. She continued to help her parents financially and contributed toward the cost of her younger brother’s high school and college expenses. As a result, Mariela gained an important decision-making role in her household. These experiences empowered Mariela. She eventually became a business owner. She opened a *tienda* (a small grocery store) and ran a taco truck. As a result, Mariela is treated with respect by her parents and the other elders in the community. As a result, she has been asked to sponsor religious processions and fiestas, a great honor usually granted to men.

Mariela is not alone. Many young women have migrated to Cancun to provide their families with an extra income. The young woman in figure 7 worked as a chambermaid in a hotel. She also dealt with similar critiques over her migration and her parents’ worry for her safety. However, she was able to squash these concerns by living with family members who vouched for her. She is now seen as a leader by her community.

**Housing Justice**

Maya women are also leaders when it comes to housing justice. In Cancun, access to housing has always been an issue. The city was built for tourists and the middle class. As a result, working class migrants have a difficult time finding affordable housing. Maya women have been at the forefront of housing justice coalitions. We can see this in the case study of Colonia Mario Villanueva, an informal settlement made up of one hundred families that was involved in a decade-long battle to avoid eviction.[^11] Magda was one of the leaders of this movement. I first met Magda when she was a child and came to know her better when I began doing research on affordable housing in Cancun. Magda lives in Colonia Mario Villanueva. She shared her struggle for housing justice in the hope that it would lead to a resolution and help others facing similar battles.

Working-class migrants have limited opportunities to gain access to affordable housing. Since rental prices are high, most Maya migrants prefer to own their homes. Prior to the 2000s, migrants could either petition the government for a subsidized land plot, purchase land or a house via the real estate market, or enter into an informal contract to purchase a land plot in an informal settlement. The latter two were the most affordable, albeit precarious, options. After the 2000s, the government began promoting social housing in the forms of tract housing developments as a solution to the housing crisis. For Magda, living in an informal settlement was precarious, but it was also the closest approximation she could get to life in her pueblo. The colonia is peppered with old growth trees, and the lots are big enough to create extensive gardens. The residents know each other and support each other during difficult times. This quality of life is difficult to nurture in tract housing developments because neighbors spend less time outside their homes, making it difficult to get to know each other. Consequently, Magda and her husband negotiated with a colonia resident to purchase their right to the informal contract in Colonia Mario Villanueva. They built their house with recycled materials and planted a garden under the tall trees that protected the property from the relentless sun.

In 2012, the neighboring garbage dump caught fire. The colonia residents were asked by the authorities to show that they had formal claims to the land. They were informed that the receipts they produced were haphazardly written and could not prove proof of ownership. They needed formalized pay statements rather than handwritten notes. Twenty-two of the colonia residents sued the landowner requesting proper documentation of their payments. In response, the landlord countersued. In 2014, The judge ruled in favor of the landowner, granting her an eviction order against the twenty-two residents. The landowner informed the colonia residents that they could avoid eviction if they paid market rate values for their plots, which the majority of the colonia residents could not afford to do. This prompted an extensive legal battle between the landowner and the majority of the colonia residents.

I began documenting the colonia’s legal battle in 2015 at Magda’s bequest. Magda became one of the lead organizers of this resistance. At twenty-three years of age and as a mother of two young children, Magda did not feel prepared to lead but she stepped in because she felt compelled to help her neighbors (fig. 8). To do so, she relied on resistance strategies deployed by the leaders of her pueblo. For Magda, Indigenous pueblos provide a blueprint for how the state should treat its citizens. She explained, “We don’t benefit from the privileges held by *pueblitos* (small pueblos). Here we need the government to intervene to regulate the colonias. In contrast, since the pueblitos are municipal headquarters, it’s easy to meet with the *comisario* (mayor), have him sign your documents, and authorize your land title.” During a visit home, she met with organizers from Antorcha Campesina (Peasant Torch), a grassroots organization with a long history of defending peasant rights that had recently begun organizing urban residents. Antorcha agreed to send two representatives to serve as mediators with political representatives from the municipal and state governments and to teach colonia residents how to organize a social movement.

This intervention came at a steep cost for leaders like Magda. In January 2015, Magda invited me to dinner at her house. I arrived at four o’clock to find a large crowd gathered at the main entrance. The landowner, accompanied by a judicial representative and a police force, informed the colonia residents that she was enforcing the eviction order for the twenty-two families. One hundred policemen in full riot gear sealed off the colonia. At this hour of the day, the majority of the men in the colonia had left to work the night shift in the hotel industry. As a result, it was mainly the women and the elderly who remained in the colonia. They sought reinforcements from their extended kin networks to form a human chain in defense of their neighbors. In the end, the families were evicted and their homes were demolished, while many of the protestors were beaten and detained by the police (fig. 9).

This eviction further motivated Magda to fight for housing justice. Magda, along with her cousin, led the colonia’s resistance and, with Antorcha’s aid, organized a public march (fig. 10). To stop the protests, the government eventually offered Antorcha land in another part of Cancun. Threats of eviction from the landlord convinced their neighbors to move out, but Magda, her cousin, and two neighbors decided to “wait out” the landowner.[^12] They continue living in the colonia today. I conclude by sharing Magda’s words about how the struggle for housing justice has shaped her life and her understanding of what it means to be a leader:

> It’s been incredibly challenging. The first weeks of the lawsuit were very difficult. It was a new experience. We had to hustle to survive on a day-to-day basis, especially when the landowner would threaten us constantly. We lived in fear. We didn’t sleep. We worried about our neighbors and made sure to take care of each other. We worried because we didn’t know when we would be evicted. We worried for our children. We had to focus on what to do in that moment. To think carefully on what to do. How to live under constant threat.
>
> I’ve done things I never expected to do in my life. I’ve slept in front of the governor’s *palacio* (mansion) in order to protest, to demand that they listen to us, to give us a solution. I’ve hustled on the streets asking for money \[on behalf of Antorcha\]. The people who pass by see that you are young and ask why do you need money? “You are young, you can work,” they say. We tell them that it’s not for us, it’s to help other students in Chetumal. \[One of Antorcha’s expectations was that colonia residents ask for funds when they leafleted the streets.\] Some people respond rudely to you and tell you no. These are the types of challenges that I never imagined I’d take on during my lifetime. To join Antorcha was another challenge. I did it in the hope that with their support, the government would resolve our problem. Sometimes we spend the entire day, without eating, through the next day at the governor’s office.
>
> Some people would drop in to be seen and then leave. If I take a shift to protest, why couldn’t they take a shift too? We are all fighting for the same reason. This resulted in many conflicts. I spent an entire week at the palacio. I didn’t go home at the cost of my personal hygiene. We stayed there because they pressured you to sleep and eat there. We had a stove, an encampment. We stayed there. Some people donated food for us to those of us who remained. We were fighting to be heard.
>
> But the governor never came out. He sent his secretary to tell us to end the protest because it was affecting Cancun, creating a poor image of the city. We were told the governor could not solve our problem, that we had to speak directly to the landowner. He explained that they couldn’t do anything more because the landowner refused to respond to their requests to meet. She told them that the government could not force her. She behaved very rudely. He told them that they couldn’t do anything.
>
> I’ve learned a lot. Enough to even go stand up and shout in front of the governor’s palace and know what my rights are and to express what I feel. I, too, am citizen, but I know that I shouldn’t break the law. So it’s like keeping a balance. But yes, I’ve learned not to be afraid to express myself. What we have lived has been shaped by what we have learned from our parents, our grandparents. To be strong, not to give up. To move forward with what is available. But to never, ever give up.
>
> When things go wrong, to not give up right away. You always have to find a way to move forward, to keep fighting for your dreams, for the goals you want to achieve. That is what makes you feel stronger every day, instead of feeling that you can’t go on any further, to believe that there is no other solution. Because there always is. There are other alternatives. The point is to keep moving forward.[^13]

Magda ended up battling for her land for over ten years. In the fall of 2025, the landowner agreed to settle the dispute. After a round of negotiations, Magda and her cousin were given purchasing agreements that will allow them to keep their land. This victory is unprecedented and is a testament to Magda and her cousin’s strength, resilience, and leadership.

**Conclusion**

The exhibition *To Take Back Our Breath: Maya Women Across Time* aims to reframe how Maya women are understood and portrayed in the popular imagination across time and space. The counter-stories I shared here disrupt depictions of Maya women as static and rural. Instead, Maya women have strategically harnessed their labor to make visible the important decision-making roles they hold in their pueblos. They also call attention to the injustices Indigenous migrants face in urban centers and within tourism economies. In so doing, they demand recognition from the state of their rights as Indigenous pueblos.[^14] Their endeavors to “move forward and never give up” speak to Maya women’s longstanding resilience in the face of Indigenous dispossession, servitude, and economic precarity. These stories of leadership aim to re*write* and re*right* Indigenous women’s experience, as Linda Tuhiwai Smith proposes, and inspire future generations of Maya women activists.[^15]
